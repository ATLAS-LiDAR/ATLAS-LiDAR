Seeing the ATLAS attack, and what makes it possible

ATLAS generates adversarial LiDAR data offline: it perturbs recorded frames and hands the result to a detector. What that perturbation actually is — which returns die, which cells a phantom can occupy, why a given point survives — stays buried in the pipeline. This figure puts it on screen. Pick a real recorded driving frame and watch the attack happen on the sensor's own scan lattice, return by return.

Two layers are on display. The perturbation itself is ATLAS: the same occlusion-aware injection, ground anchoring and range-conditioned intensity, reading the same measured beam geometry. Around it sits the part ATLAS leaves out — it assumes a synchronized attacker — so time of flight, synchronization and the receiver's angular leakage are modelled here explicitly. That is what lets the figure say not just what the attack does, but whether it is physically possible, and how far it sits from what has been demonstrated on real hardware.
